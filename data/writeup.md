# Parking Tickets Follow the Renters: A District-Level Look at LA Citations, 2022 to 2025

*Renter-heavy council districts receive roughly 50% more tickets per household for every 10 points of renter share.*

## Intro

In June, I read a r/BayArea post by u/viziomas that analyzed 659,000 San Francisco street-sweeping tickets and found that 90% are issued within the first 45 minutes of a posted two-hour window. It inspired me to do a similar analysis for Los Angeles, looking at how parking citations intersect with housing and infrastructure. Growing up in a lower-income, predominantly Hispanic neighborhood, multi-generational and multi-family households were the norm, which meant more cars competing for limited curb space. Today, rising housing costs are top of mind for Angelenos everywhere. That led me to ask: how do LA parking ticket patterns relate to housing costs, affordable housing, and access to public transit and infrastructure? In simpler terms, are lower-income, denser neighborhoods hit harder by citations?

## Data and methods

This analysis uses publicly available data from the City of Los Angeles, the Census Bureau, and Zillow. From the City of Los Angeles data portal, I used all parking citations issued between 2022 and 2025, along with the council district boundaries shapefile to determine which district each ticket was issued in. I also used the Census ACS 5-year 2024 survey for social, economic, housing, and demographic information at the block group and tract level, and Zillow for the average annual rent at the ZIP code level. I kept every ZIP in Los Angeles County, since Zillow labels some ZIPs by neighborhood or neighboring city and filtering on the city name would drop ZIPs that hold city households. A ZIP's annual rent is the average of its monthly values, and I only used a ZIP-year if at least half of that year's months were available.

I used Python's GeoPandas library to build the panel data, joining the parking, rent and Census data at the block group level and then allocating it to the district level. To make sure households are not counted more than once, I used area-weighted interpolation at the block group level, so the district totals sum to 3,855,903 people and 1,438,336 households. ZIP rents were carried to districts with household weights, and a district's rent is only reported when the ZIPs with rent data cover at least 70% of its households. I normalized the citation counts by household as the primary rate, and by person and by vehicle as secondary checks.

The Spearman rank correlation is my first measure of how ticket rates move with district characteristics. It doesn't assume a linear relationship and is less affected by a single extreme district, which matters with a small sample (15 council districts). I calculated the p-values with a permutation test, since the usual large-sample approximations may not hold with so few districts. The test shuffles one variable across the districts 10,000 times to see how often a correlation as strong as the observed one, in either direction, shows up by chance. I tested tickets per household and the change in tickets from 2022 to 2025 against income and household size, and against renter, multi-unit, rent burden and transit shares. I grouped the tests into families and applied a Bonferroni correction within the primary family (4 tests) and the housing and transit family (10 tests). The secondary checks are reported without correction. Because I chose the housing and transit tests after seeing the earlier results, I treat them as exploratory. For every test, I also dropped one district at a time to see whether the direction of the correlation stayed the same.

To see whether the strongest result holds up after accounting for other factors, I fit a negative binomial regression on the district-year ticket counts, using the log of households as an offset so the results read as changes in tickets per household. I chose the negative binomial over a Poisson model because the ticket counts vary far more than a Poisson model allows, and I estimated the dispersion from the data. Since each district appears in four years and the district characteristics don't change from year to year, I clustered the standard errors by district. With only 15 clusters, these standard errors are likely too optimistic, so I lean on the permutation tests for the main claim and use the regression as a supporting check.

## Test families

To keep the number of tests under control, I grouped them into three families, and each family is reported differently depending on how it was chosen. Because there are only 15 districts, the regression is also limited to renter share plus one control at a time (income or household size) to avoid over-fitting.

The primary family has 4 tests, which look at income and household size against average tickets per household and the change in tickets from 2022 to 2025. These were the main questions of the project, and I applied a Bonferroni correction across the 4 tests. The secondary family is made up of diagnostic checks. They compare tickets per person and tickets per vehicle against income and household size, and tickets per household and the change in tickets against 2025 rent. Rent is only available for 14 districts, since I left out District 7 because the Zillow data covered fewer than 70% of its households. These secondary tests are reported without a correction, so they are better read as supporting checks. The per-person diagnostic was included to separate a real household size effect from the fact that tickets per household equal tickets per person times household size.

The third family covers housing and transit. It compares tickets per household and the change in tickets against renter share, multi-unit share, rent burden, severe rent burden and transit commute share. This gives 10 tests, with a Bonferroni correction across all 10. Because I defined this family after seeing the earlier results, it is exploratory, and any finding from it should be treated as something to follow up on, not a confirmed result.

## Results

### Overview

Ticket rates differ a lot across the 15 council districts. Tickets per household range from 0.26 per year in District 12 to 2.86 in District 14, an 11-fold gap. Tickets are also concentrated: Districts 14, 13 and 5 account for 37% of all citations between 2022 and 2025. Citywide, the number of tickets did not change much over the period, going from about 1.97 million in 2022 to 2.03 million in 2025, an increase of only 2.8%. Five of the 15 districts saw fewer tickets in 2025 than in 2022, and the changes by district range from a 14% decline in District 13 to a 16% increase in District 4. So the main story is about where tickets are given, not how fast they are growing.

### Primary tests

None of the four primary tests were significant after the Bonferroni correction (corrected p-values from 0.65 to 1.0). Districts with higher median income and larger households tended to have fewer tickets per household (rho = -0.38 and -0.37), and the same was true for ticket growth against income (rho = -0.39). These relationships are weak and noisy, though, with the trend lines explaining only 10% and 8% of the variation in the two per-household plots (Figure 1). The direction of the first three results held when any single district was dropped, while ticket growth against household size showed no relationship (rho = 0.07). One exploratory check is worth noting: tickets per person fall more clearly as household size rises (rho = -0.68, uncorrected p = 0.008). This suggests that larger households are not getting more tickets per person, but it was a secondary test and is not part of the corrected results.

### Housing and transit

The housing and transit family produced the one result that holds up after correction. Tickets per household rise with the share of renters in a district (rho = 0.78, corrected p = 0.012), and the direction stays the same when any one district is removed. The renter share trend explains about 55% of the variation in district ticket rates, much more than income or household size (Figure 2). Multi-unit share (rho = 0.63) and transit commute share (rho = 0.65) point the same way but do not pass the correction (corrected p = 0.18 and 0.13), so I treat them as suggestive. These three measures likely describe the same kind of district, dense and apartment-heavy, and should not be read as three separate effects. District 14 sits above most of the trend lines, which suggests that something else is driving its rate, such as visitors and commuters, since my rates only count residents.

### Regression

To see whether the renter share result is just a stand-in for income or household size, I fit the negative binomial model described above. Each 10-point increase in renter share goes with about 47% to 56% more tickets per household depending on the controls (a rate ratio of 1.56 with a 95% confidence interval of 1.37 to 1.77 when income is included). Income and household size were not significant once renter share was in the model, and dropping any one district keeps the rate ratio between 1.49 and 1.62.

I used a negative binomial over a Poisson model because the ticket counts vary far more than a Poisson model allows. The Poisson fit has a dispersion statistic of about 18,000 compared to about 1.2 for the negative binomial, where a value near 1 is what a well-fitting model looks like. The dispersion parameter (alpha, about 0.12) was estimated by maximum likelihood, by fitting the model across a range of alpha values and keeping the one with the best fit. The Poisson model gives somewhat smaller rate ratios (1.33 to 1.44), since it gives more weight to districts with large counts, but the direction and rough size agree.

### What didn't work

Not all of the housing measures behaved as I expected. Rent burden runs in the opposite direction from a "higher housing costs, more tickets" story: districts with more cost-burdened renters have fewer tickets per household (rho = -0.54), and this does not hold up after correction (corrected p = 0.43). Rent in 2025 was also not related to ticket rates (rho = -0.16). Nothing I tested explained ticket growth either. Renter share (rho = 0.30) and transit share (rho = 0.35) had the strongest relationships with the change in tickets, and neither was close to significant (p = 0.28 and 0.22). I think this means the housing and transit measures explain where tickets are concentrated, but not why some districts saw tickets go up or down.

## Interpretation

Renter share, multi-unit share and transit use are probably telling the same story. Districts with many renters also tend to have more apartment buildings and more people commuting by transit, because all three describe dense, apartment-heavy parts of the city. I tested them as separate predictors, but with only 15 districts I can't pull them apart, so I read them as one "dense, apartment-heavy" pattern, not three independent effects. I model renter share because it was the strongest and the only result that survived correction. That shouldn't be read as renting itself driving tickets. It may just be the cleanest measure of that pattern.

District 14 is the clearest outlier. It has the highest ticket rate per household in the city, and it sits above the trend in most of my scatter plots. My rates divide tickets by the people, households and vehicles that live in a district, but District 14 contains downtown and other areas that draw visitors, workers and commuters. They get ticketed without being counted in the denominator, which would inflate the district's rate without saying anything about its residents. I haven't tested this directly, so it is a likely explanation, not a finding. The leave-one-out check does show that the renter result doesn't depend on any single district.

The results fit a modest version of the housing-cost argument and stop short of a stronger one. Districts with more renters receive more tickets per household, about 56% more for every 10 points of renter share, holding income constant. That is consistent with the idea that housing and parking pressure shape where tickets are issued. But rent burden doesn't support it, so I can't say that districts where housing costs more relative to income get more tickets. Ticket growth from 2022 to 2025 also remains unexplained. The data supports "where tickets are concentrated" and says little about "why they are growing."

## Limitations

**Sample size.** There are only 15 council districts, so the tests have low power. Only large associations can be detected, and a real but moderate effect could easily have been missed. This is the main reason I treat the non-significant results as inconclusive, not as evidence of no relationship. The regression has the opposite problem. I cluster standard errors by district, but with only 15 clusters they tend to be too small. The confidence interval around the renter-share rate ratio (1.37 to 1.77) is therefore probably too narrow, and the permutation test is the more conservative number.

**Resident-only denominators.** Every rate divides by the residents, households or vehicles in the district. Visitors, workers and through traffic also get tickets, and they vary a lot across districts. Where a district draws many non-residents, its rate overstates how often its residents are ticketed. District 14 is the likely example.

**Static predictors.** The census and housing measures don't change across the four years, so the analysis compares districts to each other. It can show that renter-heavy districts have higher ticket rates. It cannot show that a district's tickets change when its renter share changes, which is also why the ticket-growth question stays open.

**Exploratory housing family.** I defined the housing and transit tests, and added the per-vehicle checks, after seeing the first results. The housing family is corrected for multiple testing within itself, but the choice of tests was not pre-declared, so I treat them as leads to follow up, not confirmed findings.

**Association, not cause.** None of this shows that renters, apartments or housing costs cause more tickets. Renter-heavy districts may differ in street density, parking supply, enforcement staffing or land use in ways I didn't measure. What the analysis does show is a consistent pattern across districts, and it is a starting point for those questions.
